# Halden Ridge Observatory: Visitor and Operations Handbook

This handbook is an invented document, written only so the evaluation has facts
that no model could know from anywhere else.

## 1. About the observatory

Halden Ridge Observatory was founded in 1974 by the astronomer Ingrid Solvang.
It stands at an altitude of 2,310 metres on the eastern shoulder of Halden
Ridge. The site was chosen because it has an average of 248 clear nights a
year, more than any other ridge in the region.

The observatory is run by the Halden Ridge Trust, a charity. Its current
director is Dr. Tomas Arvelo, who took up the post in 2019.

## 2. Telescopes

The main instrument is the Solvang Reflector, a telescope with a primary mirror
1.6 metres across. It was installed in 1981 and its mirror was recoated in 2016.
The mirror is recoated with aluminium every eight years.

The second instrument is the Kestrel Refractor, a 40-centimetre telescope
built in 1958 and moved to Halden Ridge in 1976. The Kestrel is the only
telescope visitors may look through themselves.

A radio dish, 12 metres across and named Marit, was added in 2003. It is used
for student projects and is not part of public tours.

## 3. Visiting

The observatory is open to visitors from Wednesday to Sunday. It is closed on
Mondays and Tuesdays for maintenance. Daytime tours start at 10:00 and 14:00
and last 75 minutes. Night viewing sessions run on Fridays and Saturdays, begin
at 21:30, and are limited to 18 visitors each.

An adult ticket for a daytime tour costs 14 crowns. A ticket for a night
viewing session costs 32 crowns. Children under 12 enter free on daytime tours
but are not admitted to night sessions. Night sessions must be booked at least
three days in advance.

If cloud cover exceeds 70 percent at 20:00, the night session is cancelled and
every ticket is refunded in full within five working days.

## 4. Rules on the ridge

White light is forbidden outdoors after sunset, because it ruins night vision
for everyone and spoils long exposures. Visitors must use red torches, which
can be borrowed free of charge at the gatehouse.

Cars must be parked in the lower car park, 600 metres below the domes. A
shuttle bus runs from the car park every 20 minutes. Only vehicles carrying
people with reduced mobility may drive up to the domes.

Drones may not be flown anywhere on the ridge. Smoking is allowed only in the
shelter beside the lower car park.

## 5. Weather and safety

Night temperatures on the ridge fall below freezing in every month except July
and August. Visitors to night sessions are told to bring a warm coat, and the
gatehouse lends blankets.

The domes are closed whenever the wind is stronger than 55 kilometres an hour,
or the humidity is above 85 percent, to protect the mirrors.

The nearest hospital is in the town of Brennvik, 31 kilometres away. A first
aid room is in the gatehouse, and at least two members of staff on every shift
hold a mountain first aid certificate.

## 6. Research and education

Research time on the Solvang Reflector is awarded twice a year, in March and
September, by a committee of five astronomers. About 60 percent of the
telescope's time goes to the study of variable stars, the observatory's
speciality since its founding.

The observatory runs a summer school for secondary school students every July.
It lasts two weeks and takes 24 students. Students at the summer school use the
Marit radio dish to map hydrogen in the Milky Way.

## 7. Contact

Bookings are taken by telephone between 09:00 and 16:00 on opening days, and
online at any time. Lost property is kept at the gatehouse for 30 days and then
given to a charity shop in Brennvik.
